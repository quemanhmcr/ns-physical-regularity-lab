# Eliminate the moment: a universal remote-strain energy law

Two independently tested physical laws now meet:

1. If the first surviving impulse moment has order `n`, a compact remote donor produces strain

`S_far ~ M_n / d^(n+4)`.

2. Packing that same surviving moment into a smooth/closed structure of size `L` costs

`E ~ M_n^2 / L^(2n+3)`.

Do not keep the intermediate moment merely because our notation introduced it.  Eliminate it.  A donor required to produce target strain `s` needs

`M_n ~ s d^(n+4)`.

Therefore

`E_required ~ s^2 d^5 (d/L)^(2n+3)`.

The striking feature is that the raw distance exponent is always **five**, independent of cancellation depth.  Multipole complexity survives only through the compactness ratio `d/L`.

If the source is allowed to occupy a fixed fraction of its distance from the target (`L/d = eta` fixed), then every cancellation depth obeys

`E_required ~ s^2 d^5`,

and the circulation scale of a geometrically similar closed-vortex carrier obeys

`Gamma_required ~ s d^2`.

This is nature simplifying the observer's multipole bookkeeping: once the physically transmitted quantity (strain) and the physical support scale are specified, the intermediate moment label disappears.

The exact ring-stack experiment normalizes single, impulse-neutral pair, and twice-cancelled quartet configurations to produce the same axial strain at the target, then computes their regularized kinetic energy.  It tests both the universal `d^5` law at fixed compactness and the residual compactness penalty `(d/L)^(2n+3)`.

This is still a canonical family, not yet a universal inequality over all Navier-Stokes fields.  The next question is whether finite total kinetic energy turns this composition law into a **physical donor horizon**: how close any structure capable of producing a prescribed singular-rate strain must lie to the target core.
